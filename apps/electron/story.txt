The Last Lighthouse

The sea had been quiet for three years. Not calm, exactly — the old men on the
pier said a sea like that was holding its breath. Either way, the fog never
lifted, and the lighthouse no longer turned.

I arrived in August with a toolbox and a letter from my grandmother, who had
kept the light for forty years before the automation. The letter said only:
"Trust the lamp, not the panel."

The panel was easy. New bulb, fresh wiring, a capacitor the size of a loaf of
bread. I powered it on and watched the beam sweep the fog like a slow question.
Everything read green. But the fog did not move.

On the third night, I dreamed of my grandmother sitting at the top of the
stairs, oiling the great lens by hand. "Machines see the weather," she said in
the dream. "Lighthouses see the sailors."

When I woke, I did the only thing the letter allowed. I shut the automation
down, climbed the spiral stairs with a kerosene lamp, and lit the old flame
behind the glass myself.

The fog did not retreat, but somewhere beyond it — far out where the water goes
grey and then goes black — a fishing boat changed course, slow as a heartbeat,
and came home.
